Harley-Davidson — history and manufacturing

Explore the American manufacturer's origins, evolving motorcycle families and modern powertrains.

From Milwaukee in 1903

Harley-Davidson was founded in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, in 1903. Its history includes the evolution of the V-twin, the FL Touring family, Sportster and Softail platforms, and later liquid-cooled Revolution Max motorcycles.

Harley-Davidson racing, engineering and custom culture

Harley-Davidson is more than cruisers and touring: its competition and custom-building history influenced some of its most distinctive motorcycles.

Flat track: KR750, XR750 and XG750R

Harley's KR750 carried the company's flat-track tradition into the postwar era. Introduced in 1970, the XR750 was developed by race manager Dick O'Brien and his team; a revised all-aluminium version followed in 1972. The XR750 became one of American racing's most successful motorcycles, associated with champions including Jay Springsteen, Scott Parker and Chris Carr, and with Evel Knievel's stunt performances.

Modern XG750R competition continued that tradition. Racing machines are not automatically road-registerable UK motorcycles.

Harley-Davidson XR750 historical feature ↗ · The Race to 700 Wins ↗

XR1000: race engineering for the road

Built in 1983 and 1984, the XR1000 used Sportster foundations with XR750-inspired cylinder heads, twin carburettors and a striking competition-style exhaust. It wasn't a homologated XR750 racer, but remains an important and collectible street-going chapter in Harley's racing story.

Explore the XR1000 history and buyer guide →

V-Rod: Revolution engine and Porsche collaboration

The VRSCA V-Rod arrived as a 2002 model with a liquid-cooled Revolution V-twin, designed with input from Porsche Engineering and informed by Harley's VR1000 road-racing project. This was a major engineering departure from conventional air-cooled Big Twins, later expanding into several VRSC variants.

Explore V-Rod models and their history →

Arlen Ness: the custom movement

Arlen Ness became one of the defining American custom motorcycle builders, transforming a 1947 Harley-Davidson Knucklehead and later developing distinctive paintwork, bodywork and billet accessories. His independent custom motorcycles and parts business helped shape the wider Harley custom scene; these are not all official factory Harley-Davidson editions.

What is CVO — Custom Vehicle Operation?

Harley-Davidson introduced its Custom Vehicle Operation (CVO) programme in 1999. CVO motorcycles are factory-built, premium editions of established models with limited production, distinctive paint, high-specification components and sometimes engines or equipment introduced ahead of the standard range. Early FXR2 and FXR3 CVO editions were limited to 900 each. CVO is a programme and a family of editions, not one motorcycle model.

The XR1000, XR1200, V-Rod, Revolution Max models and numbered Icons Collection motorcycles illustrate different paths from competition, engineering and custom culture into production. For buying and export, always distinguish a factory model, dealer-customised machine and independent custom build.

Battistinis Custom Cycles: a British connection to Harley custom culture

Independent builders helped define the looks and engineering possibilities of the Harley-based custom scene. Their motorcycles are not necessarily factory editions.

The Anglo-American FXR custom story

In a first-hand Hot Bike feature, Battistinis described bringing British and Californian custom ideas together around the Harley-Davidson FXR. Their aim was a long, low, clean-lined motorcycle that retained the FXR's rideability rather than simply building a showpiece. It is a useful example of how individual workshops reshaped Harley style outside the factory.

Read Battistinis' FXR story in Hot Bike ↗

Independent custom builders versus factory CVO

Battistinis builds, Arlen Ness customs and other workshop specials can have bespoke frames, bodywork, engines and finishes. Such modifications must be documented individually. Factory CVO editions have their own Harley-Davidson model identity and production specifications. An S&S-powered or Screamin’ Eagle-modified custom is not automatically a CVO.

S&S Cycle, Screamin’ Eagle and Harley-Davidson performance

Three names that matter to collectors and custom builders — but they do not mean the same thing.

S&S Cycle · Independent V-twin engineering since 1958

George J. Smith and Stanley Stankos founded S&S in 1958, initially making performance components for Harley-Davidson engines. The company grew into a respected independent supplier of carburettors, stroker kits, complete engines and racing technology. An S&S-powered custom is not automatically a Harley-Davidson factory specification.

S&S Cycle company history ↗ · S&S Cycle official website ↗

Screamin’ Eagle · Harley-Davidson factory performance

Screamin’ Eagle is Harley-Davidson's own performance-parts brand, with intake, exhaust, camshaft, tuning and engine upgrade programmes. Stage I–IV packages describe different levels of modification, not a separate motorcycle model. Fitment, warranty implications and road legality depend on the specific year, parts and market.

Harley-Davidson Screamin’ Eagle performance range ↗

How Screamin’ Eagle relates to CVO

Harley-Davidson launched Custom Vehicle Operations in 1999 with the limited-production FXR2 and FXR3. Many early factory CVO editions carried Screamin’ Eagle model names. In 2009 CVO became the primary model branding, while Screamin’ Eagle continued as the factory performance-parts brand.

Why modifications matter when buying

Ask whether the motorcycle has Harley-Davidson factory equipment, dealer-installed Screamin’ Eagle parts, S&S aftermarket engine components or a complete independent custom engine. Check invoices, engine identification, original parts, insurance, noise and emissions compliance, service support and import eligibility. None of these upgrades alone establishes CVO authenticity.

Harley-Davidson through the generations

Landmarks from Harley-Davidson's own published history. These are selected introductions, not production end dates for every model.

1903–1909 · Origins of the V-twin

The company was established in Milwaukee in 1903. Harley-Davidson's first 45-degree V-twin appeared in 1909, establishing an enduring engine layout.

1936–1948 · Knucklehead and Panhead

The overhead-valve Knucklehead arrived in 1936. The aluminium-head Panhead followed in 1948, creating two landmark Big Twin engine families.

1957 · Sportster begins

The XL Sportster launched in 1957 with the Ironhead engine, following the earlier K-model family. Later Sportsters evolved considerably, so equipment must be checked by exact model year.

2017 · Milwaukee-Eight Touring

Milwaukee-Eight V-twins entered Touring production for model year 2017. They are not interchangeable with older Twin Cam specifications.

2021 onwards · Revolution Max

The liquid-cooled Revolution Max platform debuted in the Pan America and Sportster S families, with a different chassis and engine architecture from traditional air-cooled Big Twins.

2026 announcement · Future US production

Harley-Davidson announced plans in June 2026 to bring North American Revolution Max production back to US facilities, targeting the transition ahead of model year 2028. This is a future plan, not a claim that every current example was built in the US.
