    BMW R 1300 GS Adventure: manual or ASA?

    BMW's Automated Shift Assistant can operate the clutch and change gears automatically on equipped bikes.     The world of motorcycling

    The right motorcycle depends on how and where it will be ridden. Explore the design, technology and riding choices that shape motorcycling.

    Design & engineering

    Why motorcycles feel different

    A sports motorcycle places greater emphasis on aerodynamic bodywork, strong braking and a committed riding position. Touring machines generally prioritise long-distance comfort, wind protection and carrying capacity. Engine layout, chassis geometry and weight distribution all contribute to how a bike behaves.

    Browse manufacturers →

    Technology & riding

    Automatic or manual?

    Most conventional motorcycles use a rider-operated clutch and gear lever. Scooters often use continuously variable transmissions, while some motorcycles offer dual-clutch or automated-shift systems. These technologies differ in how they change gear and how much control the rider retains.

    Browse Honda models →

    International perspectives

    One bike, different countries

    A motorcycle suited to open-road touring in one country may be used for city commuting or rural travel elsewhere. Climate, road surfaces, infrastructure, licensing rules and parts availability shape how people choose and use motorcycles around the world.

    See international freight options →

    What makes a useful motorcycle comparison?

    Engine capacity alone rarely tells the whole story. An informative comparison considers the correct production years and variants, riding position, fuel capacity, weight measured on a consistent basis, available electronic equipment, transmission choices and the jobs each motorcycle is designed to do.

    Where real riders and reviewers disagree, their impressions should be credited as opinions rather than presented as technical facts. Different riding impressions should be attributed to their original reviewers.

    The riders, engineers and motorcycles that shaped competition. These historical features are based on manufacturer archives, not claims about current teams or results.

    Yamaha · Grand Prix · 1978–1980

    Kenny Roberts: three titles and a new way to race

    When American dirt-track champion Kenny Roberts joined Yamaha’s 500cc Grand Prix effort in 1978, his cornering technique brought a fresh approach to the world championship. Yamaha’s racing archive records three successive 500cc riders’ titles in 1978, 1979 and 1980, on successive YZR500 works machines. The 1978 YZR500 (0W35K) incorporated Yamaha’s YPVS exhaust-valve technology.

    Honda · Engineering · 1966–1970

    From Grand Prix technology to the CB750

    Honda’s 1966 500cc racing experience helped shape the road-going CB750 Four. Its four-cylinder engine and front disc brake became landmarks of the production motorcycle. Honda records a Suzuka 10 Hours victory for Morio Sumiya and Tetsuya Hishiki in 1969, followed by Dick Mann’s Daytona 200 victory on a CB750 in 1970. It is a story of racing knowledge finding its way into a motorcycle customers could buy.

    Yamaha & Suzuki · World GP · 1978

    One season, two manufacturers, a fiercely contested title

    The 1978 500cc world championship put Yamaha’s Kenny Roberts against Suzuki’s Barry Sheene. Yamaha’s season record places Roberts first with 110 points and Sheene second with 100. Yet Suzuki won the manufacturers’ championship that year. The distinction shows why AnyBike will track riders’ titles and constructors’ titles separately rather than treating a winning rider as proof of a manufacturer sweep.
